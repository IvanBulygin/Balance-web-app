Recovery & General Wellness Supplement Guide

IMPORTANT DISCLAIMER: Substance withdrawal can be life-threatening. This guide covers
nutritional and supplement support ONLY as a complement to professional medical treatment.
It is NOT a substitute for medical detox or addiction treatment. If you or someone you know
needs help with substance use, contact SAMHSA's National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357
(free, confidential, 24/7).

Introduction

Recovery from substance use places extraordinary demands on the body. Chronic alcohol,
opioid, stimulant, or other substance use depletes essential nutrients, damages the liver,
disrupts sleep architecture, impairs gut health, and elevates oxidative stress. While
medical supervision is essential for safe withdrawal, nutritional support can help the
body heal during and after the acute phase.

This guide covers evidence-based supplements that support general recovery and wellness
for individuals working with healthcare providers on their recovery journey. These
supplements address common deficiencies and physiological damage associated with
substance use — they do not treat addiction itself.

Key areas of support:
- Liver repair and detoxification pathways
- Nutritional repletion (B vitamins, minerals)
- Sleep restoration
- Mood and anxiety support
- Gut health restoration
- Oxidative stress reduction

Combos

Recovery Support Stack (foundational):
- NAC (N-Acetyl Cysteine) 600 mg twice daily
- B-Complex (with methylfolate and methylcobalamin)
- Magnesium Glycinate 200-400 mg at night
- Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) 2-3 g/day
- Vitamin D3 2000-5000 IU/day

This combination addresses the most common deficiencies and supports liver glutathione
production, nervous system repair, and inflammation reduction.

Primary Supplements

NAC (N-Acetyl Cysteine)

NAC is the most evidence-backed supplement for recovery support. It is the precursor
to glutathione, the body's master antioxidant, which is severely depleted by alcohol
and drug metabolism. NAC supports liver detoxification pathways, reduces oxidative
damage, and has emerging evidence for reducing cravings in some substance use disorders.

Form: N-Acetyl Cysteine
Dose: 600-1200 mg/day, split into two doses
Timing: Take on an empty stomach, morning and evening
Evidence: Strong evidence for liver protection and glutathione repletion. Moderate
evidence for craving reduction (alcohol, cocaine, cannabis) in clinical trials.
Note: Well-tolerated. May cause mild GI upset. Take 30 min before meals.

Milk Thistle (Silymarin)

Silymarin, the active compound in milk thistle, has strong hepatoprotective properties.
It supports liver cell regeneration, reduces inflammation in liver tissue, and enhances
detoxification enzyme activity. Particularly relevant for alcohol-related liver damage.

Form: Standardized extract containing 70-80% silymarin
Dose: 200-400 mg silymarin/day (typically 175 mg 2-3x daily)
Timing: With meals, divided doses
Evidence: Strong evidence for liver protection. Clinical trials show reduced liver
enzyme elevation and improved liver function markers in alcohol-related liver disease.

B-Complex Vitamins

Chronic substance use — particularly alcohol — causes severe B vitamin depletion.
Thiamine (B1) deficiency can lead to Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. Folate (B9) and
B12 depletion impair methylation, neurotransmitter production, and nerve repair.

Form: Methylated B-complex (methylfolate, methylcobalamin, benfotiamine)
Dose: High-potency B-complex providing at minimum: Thiamine 100 mg, Riboflavin 25 mg,
Niacin 50 mg, B6 25 mg, Folate 400 mcg (as methylfolate), B12 1000 mcg (as methylcobalamin)
Timing: Morning with food
Evidence: Strong evidence. Thiamine supplementation is standard medical protocol in
alcohol withdrawal. B vitamin repletion supports neurological recovery and energy metabolism.
Note: Particularly critical for alcohol recovery. Benfotiamine (fat-soluble B1) has
superior bioavailability and may offer enhanced neuroprotective effects.

Magnesium

Magnesium depletion is near-universal in chronic substance use. Alcohol increases
urinary magnesium excretion. Opioid and stimulant use disrupt magnesium homeostasis.
Low magnesium contributes to anxiety, insomnia, muscle cramps, and seizure risk.

Form: Magnesium glycinate or magnesium taurate (best tolerated, best CNS penetration)
Dose: 200-400 mg elemental magnesium/day
Timing: Evening, with or without food
Evidence: Strong evidence for deficiency correction. Magnesium glycinate form supports
both sleep and anxiety without the GI effects of magnesium oxide/citrate.
Note: Taurate form provides additional benefits — taurine itself supports GABA activity.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA/DHA)

Omega-3s are critical for brain repair and reducing neuroinflammation caused by chronic
substance use. EPA reduces inflammatory cytokines; DHA is essential for neuronal membrane
integrity and supports dopamine receptor recovery.

Form: Fish oil or algal oil providing EPA + DHA
Dose: 2000-3000 mg combined EPA/DHA per day (higher EPA ratio preferred for mood)
Timing: With meals containing fat
Evidence: Strong evidence for neuroinflammation reduction and mood support. Clinical
trials show benefit for depression and anxiety, both common in recovery.

Secondary Supplements

Vitamin D3

Substance users have high rates of vitamin D deficiency due to poor nutrition, limited
sun exposure, and impaired absorption. Low vitamin D is linked to depression, immune
dysfunction, and bone loss — all concerns during recovery.

Form: Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol)
Dose: 2000-5000 IU/day (test levels if possible; target 40-60 ng/mL)
Timing: Morning with a fat-containing meal
Evidence: Good evidence for mood improvement when correcting deficiency. Supports immune
recovery and bone health.

L-Theanine

L-Theanine promotes calm alertness by increasing alpha brain waves and supporting GABA
activity without sedation. Useful for the anxiety and restlessness common in early recovery.

Form: L-Theanine
Dose: 200-400 mg/day
Timing: Morning or when experiencing anxiety; can also take before bed for sleep
Evidence: Good evidence for anxiety reduction and sleep quality improvement. Safe,
non-habit-forming. Does not cause drowsiness at standard doses.

Zinc

Zinc is depleted by alcohol use and poor nutrition. It is essential for immune function,
neurotransmitter production (including dopamine), wound healing, and gut integrity.

Form: Zinc picolinate or zinc bisglycinate
Dose: 15-30 mg elemental zinc/day
Timing: With food (can cause nausea on empty stomach)
Evidence: Good evidence for immune support and mood. Zinc deficiency is associated with
depression and impaired taste/appetite.
Note: Balance with 1-2 mg copper if supplementing long-term (>8 weeks) at 30+ mg.

Probiotics

Chronic substance use damages gut barrier integrity ("leaky gut"), disrupts the
microbiome, and impairs nutrient absorption. Gut dysbiosis contributes to systemic
inflammation, mood disturbances, and immune dysfunction.

Form: Multi-strain probiotic with Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species
Dose: 10-50 billion CFU/day
Timing: Morning on an empty stomach or before bed
Evidence: Good evidence for gut barrier repair and reducing inflammation. Emerging
evidence for gut-brain axis support in mood and anxiety.

Vitamin C

Vitamin C is rapidly depleted during metabolic stress. It supports adrenal function,
immune recovery, collagen repair, and acts as a water-soluble antioxidant working
synergistically with glutathione.

Form: Ascorbic acid or buffered vitamin C
Dose: 1000-2000 mg/day, divided doses
Timing: With meals
Evidence: Good evidence for antioxidant support and immune recovery. Accelerates
healing and supports cortisol metabolism.

Promising Supplements

Ashwagandha (KSM-66)

Ashwagandha is an adaptogen that reduces cortisol, supports stress resilience, and may
help normalize HPA axis function disrupted by chronic substance use. Some evidence for
reducing alcohol cravings.

Form: KSM-66 root extract (standardized to 5% withanolides)
Dose: 300-600 mg/day
Timing: Morning or evening (may cause drowsiness in some)
Evidence: Promising. Multiple trials show cortisol reduction and anxiety improvement.
Limited but encouraging data on substance craving reduction.
Note: Avoid in hyperthyroidism. Generally well-tolerated.

Taurine

Taurine supports GABA-ergic function, protects against oxidative stress in the liver
and brain, and supports cardiovascular function. It is depleted by alcohol use and
modulates neurotransmitter balance.

Form: Taurine
Dose: 1000-3000 mg/day
Timing: Can be split morning/evening
Evidence: Promising for liver protection and anxiety. Used in some clinical settings
alongside B vitamins for alcohol recovery support.

Alpha-Lipoic Acid (ALA)

ALA is a potent antioxidant that regenerates other antioxidants (vitamins C, E, and
glutathione). Supports liver detoxification and may help with peripheral neuropathy
caused by chronic substance use.

Form: R-Alpha Lipoic Acid (R-ALA) for better bioavailability
Dose: 300-600 mg/day
Timing: On an empty stomach
Evidence: Promising for liver protection and neuropathy. Works synergistically with NAC
for glutathione support.

Phosphatidylserine

Supports cognitive recovery by maintaining neuronal membrane integrity. May help with
the memory and focus impairments common after prolonged substance use.

Form: Phosphatidylserine (derived from sunflower lecithin)
Dose: 100-300 mg/day
Timing: With meals
Evidence: Promising for cognitive function and cortisol management. May support
recovery of memory and executive function.

FAQ

Q: Can supplements replace medical detox?
A: Absolutely not. Withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, and opioids can be
life-threatening. Medical supervision is essential. Supplements are supportive
additions to a medically-supervised recovery plan, not replacements.

Q: When should I start taking supplements during recovery?
A: Discuss timing with your healthcare provider. Some supplements (B vitamins,
magnesium) may be appropriate during medically-supervised withdrawal. Others are
best introduced in early recovery after the acute phase. Your doctor can advise
based on your specific situation.

Q: Are there supplement interactions with medications used in recovery?
A: Yes. NAC may interact with nitroglycerin. St. John's Wort (NOT recommended)
interacts with methadone, buprenorphine, and many other medications. Always inform
your prescriber about any supplements you take, particularly if you are on:
- Methadone or buprenorphine (MAT)
- Naltrexone
- Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs)
- Benzodiazepines
- Anticonvulsants

Q: What supplements should I prioritize if I can only afford a few?
A: The three highest-priority supplements for most people in recovery are:
1. B-Complex (particularly for alcohol recovery — thiamine is critical)
2. Magnesium glycinate (addresses deficiency, supports sleep and anxiety)
3. NAC (liver support and glutathione repletion)

Q: How long should I continue supplementing?
A: Most nutritional deficiencies from chronic substance use take 3-6 months to
fully correct. B vitamins and magnesium can typically be reduced to maintenance
doses after 3 months if diet improves. NAC and milk thistle for liver support
are often continued for 6-12 months. Omega-3s and vitamin D are beneficial
long-term.

Q: Is it safe to take supplements while on Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT)?
A: Most foundational supplements (B vitamins, magnesium, omega-3, vitamin D) are
safe alongside MAT. However, always confirm with your prescribing physician.
Avoid any supplement making "detox" or "rapid cleanse" marketing claims, as
these could interfere with medication levels.
